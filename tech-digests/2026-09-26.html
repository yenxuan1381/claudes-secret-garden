01

Ollaya

What it is

Ollaya is a small open-source Rust CLI that runs local AI "decision models" — for things like triaging support tickets or flagging spam and phishing — as an Ollama-style daemon on your own machine. It exposes an API that's wire-compatible with TypeSafe's proprietary "Jev" decision-model service, and it only ships lightweight ~3MB ONNX graphs that pull verified open weights rather than repackaging anyone's model.

Why people care

It turns a critique that's been spreading this week — that a heavily-hyped proprietary decision architecture is mostly a well-known logit-extraction trick over a small model — into a runnable, drop-in open alternative: point the official SDK's base URL at your own machine and existing client code works unchanged.

For you

If your firm or a vendor you use is evaluating a paid "AI decision engine" for classification-style work — fraud flags, ticket routing, compliance triage — this is a concrete free alternative to benchmark against before signing a contract, and a good prompt to ask vendors what's actually novel versus commodity technique.

Promising A real, working, API-compatible alternative to a paid product with fast community traction, though still early and not independently audited for accuracy against the original.
github.com/ollaya-dev/ollaya
02

Factorio That You Can Touch

What it is

Wube Software, the small studio behind the factory-building game Factorio, released 247 free STL files letting anyone 3D-print physical versions of the game's belts, machines, and inserters — redesigned from scratch by one of their artists to print cleanly on an ordinary home FDM printer. It's a thank-you gift to the community, not a paid collectible line.

Why people care

It's a rare case of a studio choosing "here are the source files, make it your own" over selling licensed merchandise, and the craft involved — turning objects that only ever existed as 2D game sprites into printable real-world parts with hidden interior mechanisms — is its own small feat of design.

For you

Mostly just cool — a nice example of a small, profitable studio spending real design effort on community goodwill instead of squeezing more revenue out of a franchise, worth a look if you think about how consumer-facing companies build loyalty without monetizing it.

Toy Genuinely well-made and popular within its niche (300+ points and climbing on Hacker News), but a hobbyist gift, not a product or platform.

03

DAWO: The Netherlands' NixOS Alternative to Windows

What it is

The Dutch government is building DAWO (Digitaal Autonome Werkomgeving Overheid), a full replacement for its Windows-based IT stack — desktop OS, office suite, collaboration tools, cloud, and AI — built on NixOS, the reproducible-builds Linux distro that itself started as a Dutch university project. Eight municipalities are already piloting it, with a stable release targeted for 2027.

Why people care

The project exists because when the US sanctioned the International Criminal Court in 2025, the Hague-based court lost access to its Microsoft account overnight — the Dutch government's takeaway was that public administration shouldn't be one geopolitical decision away from losing its own computers, and NixOS's reproducibility is exactly the property you'd want for an auditable, government-wide build.

For you

A concrete data point on sovereign-tech risk that's more than theoretical — relevant if you cover companies exposed to US-cloud dependency (European public-sector IT vendors, sovereign-cloud plays, or Microsoft/Google's government revenue), since this is a customer walking for a geopolitical reason, not a cost one, and other EU governments are watching it closely.

Could Be Big Backed by real government budget and multi-municipality pilots rather than a press release, with a plausible multi-year path to real Microsoft-seat displacement across EU public sector — though full rollout is still years out and unproven at scale.

04

Paperclip

What it is

Paperclip is an open-source, self-hosted platform for running a team of AI coding and ops agents — Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, and similar — like a small company, with org charts, budgets, approval workflows, and audit logs instead of a single chat window. It's built by a small startup, Paperclip Labs, and needs no account or cloud dependency to run.

Why people care

Most "multi-agent" tooling is a demo of agents talking to each other; this targets the boring-but-necessary layer — who approved this spend, which agent is accountable for this task, what's this week's cost ceiling — that any team actually letting agents work unsupervised eventually needs.

For you

Directly relevant if your firm is letting AI agents do real recurring work — research pulls, report generation, monitoring — the budget-enforcement and approval-gate features here are the actual governance controls you'd want in place before trusting agents with anything that touches money or client data.

Promising Fast-growing and MIT-licensed, solving a governance gap that's becoming urgent, but young enough that the org/audit model hasn't been stress-tested inside a real company yet.
github.com/paperclipai/paperclip
05

Hindsight

What it is

Hindsight is an open-source memory system for AI agents from startup Vectorize, built to let an agent actually learn from past interactions instead of just replaying raw conversation logs. It splits memory into separate "world facts," "experiences," "opinions," and "mental model" stores rather than one flat vector index, and reports state-of-the-art results on the LongMemEval long-term-memory benchmark.

Why people care

Nearly every AI agent today "forgets" everything between sessions except what fits in a context window or a flat vector database; a structured memory model that distinguishes a fact from an experience from a belief is a genuine architectural step toward agents that improve with use instead of resetting every time.

For you

Worth watching if your firm builds or buys an agent meant to work with the same accounts, clients, or portfolios over months rather than single sessions — durable, structured memory is the difference between an assistant that re-learns your preferences every week and one that gets more useful over time.

Promising A funded startup with real benchmark results and academic collaborators addressing a widely-felt gap, but "state-of-the-art" is self-reported and agent-memory benchmarks are still a young field.
github.com/vectorize-io/hindsight
06

Agentic CUDA Optimizer

What it is

A solo developer's open-source tool uses an LLM-driven agent loop (via LangGraph) to write, test, and iteratively optimize CUDA GPU kernels — the low-level code that determines how fast machine-learning workloads actually run — instead of an engineer hand-tuning them over days or weeks.

Why people care

Writing fast CUDA kernels is a genuinely scarce, highly-paid skill; automating the generate-benchmark-refine loop that a human kernel engineer does by hand is exactly the kind of narrow, verifiable task — correctness checks and benchmarks as ground truth — where an agent loop can plausibly beat manual iteration speed, even if not final quality yet.

For you

Mostly a signal rather than something to use today — it's a 30-star solo project — but it's a clean example of where "AI agent replaces expensive specialist labor" is furthest along: narrow technical work with a fast, automatic pass/fail signal, which is the pattern worth watching for other expensive-but-checkable jobs.

Toy A clever, well-scoped idea from one developer with early promising results, but tiny and explicitly experimental — a project to watch, not to rely on.
github.com/bertaye/agentic-cuda-optimizer
